Records and recorded richness

Where are most birds recorded?

Each circle represents one state or territory. Circle area scales with its observation record count. Larger circles indicate more submitted records.

Queensland has the largest state total. About 1.47 million observation records were submitted. Queensland and New South Wales contribute 54% together.

More observers and trips can increase record counts. One record represents one sighting, regardless of individual count. Mapped state totals include 5,243,943 records. Six records have unknown states and are excluded.

Which states record more bird species?

Each state is compared using 100,000 observation records. Darker green indicates more distinct recorded species. Equal record counts reduce differences in recording effort.

Queensland leads the equal-record comparison. About 529 species appear per 100,000 records. That exceeds New South Wales by 86.5 species.

Colours show means across 200 samples per state. Each sample contains 100,000 records, without replacement. Hover for the 5th–95th percentile range. Observer bias remains.

Species and local variation

Compare the same four birds across states and territories. Darker cells indicate higher recording rates. Rates use species records per 1,000 state records. Hover for exact rates and counts.

Rainbow Lorikeet rates differ strongly between states. New South Wales records 7.3 times Tasmania’s rate. The two labelled cells show this comparison.

Rates account for differences in overall state recording activity. They do not measure bird populations.

How does local bird richness vary within each state?

Each dot represents one 1° grid cell. Boxes cover the middle 50% of cell values. The dark line marks the median, or middle value. Capped whiskers mark the minimum and maximum richness.

Victoria’s median exceeds Tasmania’s by about 30 species. Richness includes every species recorded within each cell. Australian Capital Territory has only two qualifying grid cells. These appear as dots without a box plot.

08 - Conclusion

What the observations can tell us

Across 5.24 million records, reporting activity varies across Australia. Recorded richness and familiar species show distinct patterns. Queensland leads the equal-record comparison at about 529 species. Each state comparison uses samples of 100,000 records.

These findings cover January–August 2025 observations. Observer visits and accessible locations shape reported patterns. Equal-record comparisons help account for differing recording effort. They do not measure abundance or complete annual coverage.
